# Methodology Notes: Key Judgment Calls

This document walks through the analytical decisions in this model that involved real ambiguity — places where the "right" number or approach wasn't obvious, and had to be reasoned through rather than looked up. If you're reviewing this project to assess analytical judgment rather than just Excel mechanics, this is the most relevant document in the repo.

## 1. The Scope Mismatch — Found Late, No Clean Fix, and Why That's the Point

The single most important judgment call in this project surfaced after the model was otherwise complete: Option A's economics run at full global Printing-segment scale (every product line, every generation, consumer and commercial combined — roughly $16.8B). Option B was originally framed as "US consumer/home." Comparing the two headline NPVs as if they were equivalently scoped alternatives is a real mismatch, not a disclosure nicety — Option A's larger base-case NPV is partly a structural artifact of touching a much bigger base, not on its own evidence that it's the "better" lever.

**One axis resolved for free.** HP Instant Ink is confirmed to operate across the US, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and most of Europe — not a US-only program. The subscriber counts, growth rates, and value-per-conversion commentary Option B was built on were almost certainly global metrics all along; "US consumer/home" was a narrative label layered on top of numbers that were never actually US-scoped. Fixed by relabeling the target "global consumer/home" — a narrative correction, no formula changes, zero cost.

**The other axis had no clean fix, and three plausible-sounding ones were each correctly rejected:**

1. **Inventing a consumer-mix % to narrow Option A down toward Option B's scope.** No public consumer-vs-commercial dollar split exists within HP's Printing segment (only a growth-rate contrast, not a revenue-mix figure). Rejected: this would have been a new, unanchored judgment call manufactured specifically to paper over a comparability problem, not to model something real.
2. **Reframing Option A as "the portfolio-wide refresh program" instead of "a single generation launch."** Rejected: HP genuinely treats each product-line generation as its own distinct go/no-go decision with its own timing and budget. This reframe would have quietly redefined the decision being modeled rather than represented it honestly.
3. **Adding an explicit, unhidden "Portfolio Share Represented by This Generation" toggle** — deliberately not asserting a value, exposing the uncertainty as an adjustable parameter instead of hiding it inside a fixed guess. This is a genuinely different, more honest kind of fix than 1 or 2 — and it was still rejected, on its own real merits: it would add a new moving part to a project that already asks a lot of its audience, trading one form of rigor (transparency about the unknown) against a real cost (comprehensibility) that wasn't worth paying at this stage.

**The resolution:** when every available fix trades one real problem for another — new unanchored numbers, or new complexity — the rigorous move is to name the boundary of what the model can know from public data, not keep searching for a clever patch. A plainly-worded scope-limitation note is carried in three places: `Cover_Assumptions` (directly in Option A's locked-assumptions block), `Portfolio_Comparison` (a visually prominent callout near the financial summary, not a buried footnote — the initial "just add a footnote" instinct was correctly judged insufficient for the size of the issue), and the deck.

**Why this is worth calling out as a story, not hidden as a flaw:** across several rounds, three plausible fixes were proposed and each was rejected for a specific, real reason rather than accepted for convenience. Recognizing that naming a genuine boundary of what public data can support is itself the rigorous answer — not a consolation prize — is arguably a harder discipline to hold under time pressure and sunk-cost pull than getting a formula right the first time.

**A late structural fix that followed from the same discovery:** the scope note was first added near the Portfolio Comparison table (slide 6 of the deck), which is too late — a reader forms a mental model on slides 2–5, including a chart that visually sums both options into one bar, well before reaching a caveat on slide 6. The fix moved scope signals to the earliest points a reader could reasonably anchor on the wrong idea: the deck's opening framing line, a small scope tag inside each option's own headline stat card, and an expanded disclaimer on the Combined Scenario slide — rather than one correction at the end. The lesson generalizes past this one deck: a caveat is only as good as how early a reader encounters it relative to when they'd otherwise form the wrong conclusion.

## 2. The Option A Supplies-Revenue Formula Bug

Every NPV in this model is driven by **incremental** cash flow (Invest Case minus Do-Nothing Case), so both sides of every subtraction need to be built on the same conceptual basis before the subtraction means anything.

Option A's Supplies Revenue line ($M) is anchored to a real dollar figure (Cover_Assumptions' FY25 Printing revenue minus hardware revenue) and then carried forward by an index. The first-draft formula had two separate bugs, both caught before the number was trusted:

- **Unit mismatch:** the anchor formula subtracted a figure stored in $B from a figure stored in $M without converting both consistently — nearly a no-op subtraction instead of the intended ~$10.9B Supplies base.
- **Double-counted bridge year:** the bridge term from the real FY25 anchor to the model's FY27 starting column was accidentally squared, applying two years of decline where only one was intended.

**Corrected formula:** `=(Cover_Assumptions!$B$11*1000-Cover_Assumptions!$B$27*1000)*(1+Cover_Assumptions!$B$13)*(B12/100)`, verified against the real FY25 Supplies decline rate.

The same category of bug recurred once more, later, in Option B's own dollar-anchoring rows (subscription and non-subscription revenue anchored to total Printing revenue instead of the Supplies-only base) — worth flagging as a pattern rather than a one-off: any formula pulling a dollar anchor from the segment-total cell needs to be checked for whether the hardware portion needs subtracting out first. Neither bug was caught by the output "looking wrong" — both looked like reasonable numbers in isolation. Both were caught by checking whether the two sides of a subtraction, or a dollar anchor and its intended base, were actually built on the same footing before trusting what came out of them.

## 3. Confirming Non-Linearity Before Choosing a Sensitivity-Grid Method — and a Toggle-Corruption Bug in the Linear Grid

Option A's two sensitivity variables (aftermarket recapture premium, NPI launch cost) are both linear in the model — the premium adds directly to a constant-base index every year with no compounding, and NPI cost is a single Year-1 subtraction. This was used deliberately: rather than building a 16×16 grid with Excel's Data Table feature, the grid was built off two derived "sensitivity rate" cells (NPV per point of recapture premium, NPV per dollar of NPI cost), verified against the known worst/best corners before building out the full grid. Faster to build and fully auditable, since every cell traces to already-verified formulas — but only valid because the underlying relationship is actually linear, which was true here and confirmed, not assumed.

**Option B's two variables (growth acceleration, CAC) are not linear** — growth acceleration compounds inside an exponent every year, and CAC multiplies against a subscriber count that itself depends on that same compounding rate. This was checked before building anything: the known Base/Base NPV was compared against the mathematical average of the two known opposite corners. A linear relationship requires these to match exactly; they were off by more than 2x, confirming real interaction between the two variables. Excel's native Data Table feature — the technically correct tool for a genuinely non-linear case, since it recalculates the real formula chain rather than needing a closed-form shortcut — was used instead, and worked cleanly.

**A real bug in Option A's linear-shortcut grid, found late:** the grid's intercept term was back-solved from the model's *live* NPV cell, which changes value depending on wherever the scenario toggles happen to be sitting — even though the formula's coordinates assumed the toggles sat at Base. Moving either toggle away from Base silently corrupted the entire grid with no error thrown, because the live cell no longer represented the Base/Base point the formula assumed. Fixed by adding a genuinely fixed, hardcoded reference cell for the verified Base-case NPV and re-deriving the intercept from that instead of the live cell. Verified afterward: toggling either dropdown away from Base no longer affects the grid at all. The Portfolio Comparison tab's own financial summary table applies the identical discipline for the same reason — its NPV figures are deliberately hardcoded, not live-linked, so the summary can't silently drift depending on wherever the option tabs' toggles happen to be sitting.

## 4. Why LTV Lift Was Dropped as a Modeled Driver — and Replaced With a Cost Toggle

Option B's real anchoring data includes two different kinds of claims from two different pieces of HP management commentary: growth acceleration ("how many convert," grounded in real historical subscriber-count trend data) and LTV lift ("how valuable each conversion is," grounded in a separate disclosure about post-conversion customer behavior). The first draft tried to model both as one combined benefit-side mechanic.

That bundling made Option B's revenue logic meaningfully harder to audit than Option A's single-lever structure, and combining two different-shaped claims into one model without doing the work to properly separate their uncertainty would have overstated the model's real precision. LTV lift was moved to an explicit, honest footnote in the workbook instead of a modeled driver — named plainly, with its source cited, and with an explicit statement that actual uplift could be meaningfully higher than what the model shows as a result of the exclusion. This is the same category of decision as excluding discounted payback period as a reported finding would be in a different model: computing or understanding something is different from reporting it as a headline number, and knowing which one is appropriate matters more than being able to compute the number at all.

Dropping LTV lift also left Option B with only one sensitized axis, when Option A has two (a cost lever and a favorability lever). Rather than leave that asymmetry in place, a CAC (cost per incremental subscriber) toggle was added — grounded in the model's own already-verified implied ARPU, not a fresh external estimate, and giving Option B real cost-side sensitivity for the first time. An alternative considered and rejected: tethering Option B's cost directly to a fraction of Option A's NPI budget toggle, which was rejected because it would create a live dependency between two options that are explicitly modeled as independent, fundable alone — a convenient shortcut that would have quietly undermined the project's own framing.

## 5. The Combined Scenario — Sizing a Real Coordination Effect Without a Unit-Count Base

Chris's real HP experience was that Option A and Option B's independence — while the right way to model each as its own fundable decision — doesn't fully reflect how the actual strategic conversation evolved: an early process treated hardware-cycle revenue and subscription growth as separate workstreams, while a later, more integrated process built an explicit subscription-attach design question directly into the hardware launch process itself. That's a real third mechanism — a new-printer buyer enrolling in Instant Ink during setup — that's genuinely absent from Option A's own model (which answers "does the ink dollar go to genuine HP," not "does this buyer become a subscriber") and genuinely distinct from Option B's actively-marketed conversion mechanic.

This was deliberately built as a third artifact — a `Combined_Scenario` tab referencing both options' already-verified outputs — rather than folded into Option A directly (rejected: real risk of destabilizing a fully verified, already bug-tested tab) or left as an unquantified narrative footnote (rejected: insufficiently rigorous given how central this dynamic was to the real underlying story). It's deliberately not called "Option C," to avoid muddying the "two independent, each fundable alone" framing that Options A and B still honor.

With no absolute unit-count base available anywhere in the model, the attach effect is sized as a dollar-to-dollar relationship instead of a rate applied to a buyer count: Option A's incremental hardware revenue × a 15% attach conversion rate, at the Supplies margin. That 15% rate is explicitly named as the softest assumption in the entire model — two steps removed from any disclosed figure — loosely bounded by a real 60% HP+-eligible-shipment figure as a ceiling on connectivity-capable buyers, but not derived from any disclosed conversion rate, and deliberately not sensitized, so the model's weakest number isn't the one given a whole grid of false precision around it.

**A correction worth noting on its own:** the first-pass intuition was that the attach effect "should" land smaller than Option B's full result, on the theory that a secondary channel is naturally smaller. That doesn't actually follow — conversion efficiency and reachable population are independent variables, and a smaller, more efficient channel can outproduce a larger, less efficient one with no default ordering. The check was corrected to a rate-level sanity bound (the attach rate shouldn't exceed the real ~60% connectivity ceiling) instead of a dollar-level comparison that presupposed which mechanism should "win."

## 6. Discount Rate Selection

The 8.0% discount rate is the midpoint of two independent, company-specific third-party WACC estimates for HPQ (7.47% and 9.0%, both CAPM-based, using HPQ's actual beta and capital structure). This is a stronger anchor than a generic industry-benchmark range would be — it's specific to the actual company being modeled, not a proxy — and is labeled explicitly as a third-party estimate midpoint, not reverse-engineered from HP's disclosed capital structure.
